## Introduction to Statistics
Statistics is a way to get information from data. Statistics is a discipline which is concerned with:

  - summarizing information to aid understanding,
  - drawing conclusions from data,
  - estimating the present or predicting the future, and
  - designing experiments and other data collection.

In making predictions, Statistics uses the concept of probability, which models chance mathematically and enables calculations of chance in complicated cases.

## Why Statistics?
Engineering problems are rarely deterministic. Measurements vary, materials behave inconsistently, and environmental conditions change. Statistics equips engineers with the ability to analyze variability, assess reliability, and make decisions under uncertainty. Statistical methods are essential for designing new products, improving existing systems, optimizing processes, and evaluating performance. Through statistical analysis, engineers can distinguish meaningful signals from random noise, assess the quality of data, and quantify uncertainty in their conclusions. Beyond technical applications, statistical thinking supports objectivity in scientific inquiry. Conclusions drawn from data are strengthened when they are supported by formal statistical reasoning rather than subjective judgment. As a result, statistics plays a central role in research, quality control, risk assessment, and evidence-based decision-making across many disciplines. Even when engineers do not directly perform statistical analyses, understanding statistical concepts allows them to interpret results critically and make informed professional judgments.

## Branches of Statistics
Statistics is traditionally divided into two interconnected branches: descriptive statistics and inferential statistics.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

### Descriptive Statistics
Descriptive statistics focuses on organizing, summarizing, and presenting data in a form that is easy to understand. This includes representing data visually through graphs and tables, as well as numerically through summary measures such as averages and measures of variability. The goal of descriptive statistics is to provide a clear and concise picture of the data at hand. At this stage, no generalizations are made beyond the observed dataset. Instead, descriptive statistics answers the question of what the data reveal about the observed phenomenon.

### Inferential Statistics
Inferential statistics extends beyond the observed data by using information from a sample to draw conclusions about a larger population. Because it is often impractical or impossible to measure an entire population, inferential methods allow us to make informed statements about population characteristics based on limited data.

Probability theory underpins inferential statistics by providing a framework for quantifying uncertainty. Through estimation and hypothesis testing, inferential statistics enables researchers and engineers to assess the strength of evidence, evaluate competing explanations, and make predictions with known levels of confidence.

## Variables and Types of Data
A variable is any characteristic or attribute that can take different values across individuals, objects, or observations. Variables form the basic building blocks of statistical analysis.

Variables can be broadly classified as qualitative or quantitative. Qualitative variables describe characteristics or categories and are typically expressed as labels or names. Examples include material type, defect category, or color classification. Quantitative variables, on the other hand, represent numerical measurements and express quantities such as weight, temperature, pressure, or time.

Quantitative variables can be further classified based on how they take values. Discrete variables arise from counting processes and take distinct, separate values, while continuous variables arise from measurement processes and can take any value within a specified range. This distinction is important because it influences the choice of statistical methods and graphical representations.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

## Univariate and Bivariate Data
Statistical data can also be classified based on the number of variables involved in the analysis. When data consist of measurements on a single variable, the data are referred to as univariate. Univariate analysis focuses on understanding the distribution, central tendency, and variability of that variable.

When two variables are measured simultaneously for each observation, the data are described as bivariate. Bivariate analysis explores relationships between variables, such as associations, trends, or dependencies. Understanding whether and how two variables are related is a key objective in many engineering and scientific studies.

## Populations and Samples
In statistics, a population refers to the complete set of all individuals, objects, or measurements of interest in a study. A sample is a subset of the population selected for analysis. Because studying an entire population is often impractical, samples are used to represent populations.

The manner in which a sample is selected is critical. Well-designed sampling procedures aim to ensure that samples are representative of the population, thereby allowing valid inference. A commonly used approach is simple random sampling, in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.

Numerical characteristics that describe a population are called parameters, while numerical characteristics computed from sample data are called statistics. For example, the population mean is denoted by the symbol $\mu$, whereas the sample mean is denoted by $x$. One of the central goals of statistics is to use sample statistics to estimate unknown population parameters.

## Graphical and Numerical Summaries of Data
Graphical displays play a crucial role in understanding data. For qualitative data, graphs such as bar charts and pie charts are commonly used to display frequencies and proportions. For quantitative data, graphical tools such as histograms, box plots, dot plots, and stem-and-leaf displays reveal important features of the distribution, including its center, spread, and shape.

In addition to graphical summaries, numerical summary statistics provide concise descriptions of data. Measures of location, such as the mean, median, and mode, describe where the data tend to cluster. Measures of variability, including the range, variance, and standard deviation, describe how spread out the data are around the center. Together, graphical and numerical summaries provide complementary perspectives on the same dataset.

## Describing Data Patterns
When analyzing data, statisticians commonly describe distributions in terms of several key features. These include the center of the distribution, the degree of variability or spread, and the overall shape of the distribution. Shape is often discussed in terms of symmetry, skewness, and the presence of multiple peaks.

Modern data analysis emphasizes not just computing summary measures but also interpreting what they reveal about the underlying process that generated the data.

## Unusual Features in Data
Real-world data often contain unusual features that merit special attention. Gaps in a distribution occur when certain ranges of values have no observations, potentially indicating structural constraints or data collection issues. Outliers are extreme values that differ markedly from the rest of the data and may arise from measurement errors, rare events, or meaningful deviations.

A commonly used rule for identifying outliers is based on the interquartile range. Observations that lie more than 1.5 times the interquartile range below the first quartile or above the third quartile are often classified as outliers. Identifying and interpreting unusual features is an important step in responsible data analysis.

## Comparing Data Sets
Comparing two or more datasets is a common task in engineering and scientific studies. Graphical tools such as side-by-side box plots, comparative histograms, and grouped bar charts allow analysts to assess similarities and differences across datasets.

When comparing distributions, attention is typically given to differences in center, variability, shape, and the presence of unusual features. A systematic comparison helps ensure that conclusions are based on evidence rather than visual impressions alone.

## Summarizing Data Graphically
One of the first and most important steps in statistical analysis is visualizing the data\textbf{.} Graphical summaries allow us to explore data quickly, detect patterns, identify unusual features, and guide further analysis. Before computing numerical summaries such as means or standard deviations, statisticians almost always begin by examining appropriate graphs.

The choice of a graphical display depends fundamentally on the type of data being analyzed. In general, different graphical tools are used for qualitative (categorical) data and quantitative (numerical) data. Using an inappropriate graph can obscure important information or lead to misleading interpretations.

### Graphical Displays for Qualitative (Categorical) Data
Qualitative data describe attributes or categories rather than numerical magnitudes. Graphs for qualitative data emphasize counts, proportions, and comparisons between categories.

The bar chart remains the most effective and widely used graphical tool for qualitative data. Each category is represented by a bar whose height corresponds to its frequency or relative frequency. Bar charts allow for easy comparison across categories and are particularly useful in engineering quality assessments, such as defect classification.

The pie chart displays each category as a sector of a circle, with sector sizes proportional to category percentages. Pie charts are useful for illustrating how categories contribute to a whole, although they are less effective when precise comparisons are required or when many categories are present.

A stacked bar chart extends the basic bar chart by showing subcategories within each bar. This type of graph is useful when comparing compositions across groups, such as defect types across multiple production lines.

A Pareto chart is a specialized bar chart in which categories are ordered from most frequent to least frequent. Often used in quality control and industrial engineering, Pareto charts help identify the most significant contributors to a problem by visually reinforcing the ``vital few versus trivial many'' principle.

The histogram is one of the most important tools for exploring quantitative data. ## Summary Statistics
While graphical displays allow us to visualize data, summary statistics provide concise numerical descriptions of key features of a dataset. Together, graphical and numerical summaries form the foundation of exploratory data analysis. Summary statistics are commonly grouped into measures of location and measures of variability\textbf{.}

\textbf{Measures of Location (Central Tendency)}

Measures of location describe where the data tend to cluster, or what might be considered a ``typical'' value in the dataset. The most commonly used measures of location are the mean, median, and mode.

The mean is the arithmetic average of the data and is widely used in engineering and scientific applications. However, it is sensitive to extreme values. The median is the middle value when the data are ordered and provides a measure of center that is resistant to outliers. The mode is the most frequently occurring value and is particularly useful for qualitative data or for identifying common outcomes.

In engineering practice, the choice of an appropriate measure of location depends on the nature of the data and the presence of unusual values. For example, median lifetimes are often preferred over means when analyzing component failure times due to skewness.

### Measures of Spread (Variability)
Measures of spread describe how dispersed the data are around the center. Two datasets may have the same mean but very different variability, leading to different engineering implications.

The range is the difference between the maximum and minimum values and provides a simple measure of spread. The variance measures the average squared deviation from the mean and forms the basis for many statistical methods. The standard deviation, which is the square root of the variance, is the most commonly used measure of variability because it is expressed in the same units as the original data.

In manufacturing and quality control, low variability is often as important as achieving a target mean, since excessive variation can lead to defects and system failure.

### Describing Data Patterns
Beyond center and spread, statisticians describe data using additional features that capture the overall pattern of the distribution. These features are most easily recognized through graphical displays

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

The shape of a distribution refers to its general appearance. A distribution may be symmetric or skewed to the left or right. Skewness indicates the direction and degree of asymmetry, while kurtosis describes the heaviness of the tails and the peakedness of the distribution. Understanding these features helps engineers interpret the underlying processes generating the data. For example, right-skewed distributions are common in waiting times and failure data, while symmetric distributions often arise from measurement error around a stable process.

## Unusual Features in Data
Real-world data rarely follow idealized patterns perfectly. As a result, statisticians pay close attention to unusual features that may indicate important phenomena, data collection issues, or rare but critical events.

The two most common unusual features are gaps and outliers.

### Gaps
A gap refers to a region within the range of the data where no observations occur. Gaps may arise due to physical constraints, changes in operating conditions, or data recording errors. In some cases, a gap may indicate the presence of distinct subgroups within the data, suggesting that a single distribution may not adequately describe the dataset.

### Outliers
An outlier is an observation that lies unusually far from the rest of the data.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

Outliers can result from measurement error, data entry mistakes, or genuinely extreme events. A commonly used rule for identifying outliers is based on the interquartile range (IQR). Observations lying more than 1.5 IQRs below the first quartile or above the third quartile are often flagged as potential outliers.

In engineering applications, outliers should never be removed automatically. Instead, they should be investigated carefully, as they may signal system faults, safety risks, or rare but important operating conditions.

## Comparing Data Sets
In many practical situations, the goal is not to analyze a single dataset but to compare two or more datasets. Graphical displays are especially powerful for this purpose, as they allow direct visual comparison across groups or conditions.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

Common graphical tools for comparison include side-by-side box plots, comparative histograms, dot plots, and bar charts. These displays help reveal differences and similarities that may not be apparent from numerical summaries alone.

### Key Features for Comparing Data Sets
When comparing multiple datasets, statisticians focus on four fundamental features. The center indicates typical values, the spread reflects variability, the shape reveals distributional characteristics such as skewness or multimodality, and unusual features highlight gaps or outliers. A systematic comparison across these features ensures that conclusions are grounded in evidence rather than visual impression or single summary measures.

## Sampling Procedures
In statistical practice, it is rarely feasible to observe every element of a population. Instead, statisticians rely on sampling procedures to select a subset of observations, called a sample, from which conclusions about the population can be drawn. The fundamental goal of any sampling procedure is to obtain a sample that is representative of the population, meaning it accurately reflects the population's key characteristics.

A sampling procedure is said to be random if every possible sample of a given size has a known and non-zero probability of selection. Random sampling protects against systematic bias and provides the theoretical foundation for statistical inference. Without randomness, probability-based statements about uncertainty and confidence are no longer valid.

Different sampling procedures are used depending on the structure of the population, the objectives of the study, and practical constraints such as cost and accessibility.

### Simple Random Sampling
Simple random sampling is the most fundamental sampling method and serves as the conceptual benchmark against which other methods are compared. In simple random sampling, every possible sample of size $n$drawn from a population of size $N$has equal probability of being selected.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

For a finite population of size $N$, there are

\[
\left(\begin{matrix}N\\n\end{matrix}\right)
\]

distinct samples of size $n$. A sample is considered a simple random sample if each of these possible samples has probability $1/(\frac{N}{n})$of being chosen.

For an infinite population, or for populations that are very large relative to the sample size, a sample is considered random if each observation is selected independently from the others and according to the same probability distribution. In practice, many large populations can be treated as infinite when the sample size is small relative to the population size.

**Example 1**
There are 26 letters in the English alphabet. The total number of distinct samples of 5 letters that can be drawn is

\[
\left(\frac{26}{5}\right)=65,780.
\]

If a procedure is designed so that each of these 65,780 possible samples has an equal probability of being selected, then the resulting sample is a simple random sample. No letter or combination of letters is favoured over another.

Simple random sampling is conceptually ideal, but in practice it can be difficult to implement when populations are large, geographically dispersed, or poorly defined.

### Systematic (Regular) Sampling
Systematic sampling is a structured alternative to simple random sampling that is often easier to implement in industrial and engineering settings. In this method, elements are selected at regular intervals from an ordered population.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

The procedure begins by randomly selecting a starting point from the first $k$elements of the population. Thereafter, every $k$-th element is selected for inclusion in the sample. The value of $k$, known as the sampling interval, is typically determined by dividing the population size by the desired sample size.

Systematic sampling has the advantage that the selected observations are spread evenly across the population, which often leads to samples that are highly representative. However, care must be taken to ensure that the population does not contain hidden periodic patterns that coincide with the sampling interval.

**Example 2**
In inspecting a batch of 1,000 pipes for defects, an engineer may choose to inspect every 10th pipe. The inspected pipes would then be the 10th, 20th, 30th, and so on, up to the 1,000th pipe.

This method is efficient and easy to implement. However, if every 10th pipe happens to be produced by a specific machine or process stage, the sample may be biased and fail to represent the entire batch accurately. Thus, systematic sampling requires careful consideration of the production process.

### Stratified Random Sampling
[Figure: see the PDF notes]

Stratified random sampling is designed to improve representativeness when a population consists of distinct subgroups that differ in important ways. In this approach, the population is divided into mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive sub-populations, called strata, and random samples are drawn independently from each stratum.

The strata are chosen so that observations within each stratum are relatively homogeneous with respect to the parameter of interest, while differences between strata are meaningful. This structure often leads to more precise estimates than simple random sampling.

**Example 3**
To study income distribution among engineers in the Bay Area, the population can be divided into strata based on engineering discipline, such as electrical, mechanical, civil, chemical, and industrial engineering. Random samples are then selected from each discipline.

This approach is justified by the reasonable assumption that income levels are influenced by specialization. Stratified sampling ensures that all major engineering fields are adequately represented in the final sample.

### Cluster Sampling
Cluster sampling is a sampling procedure in which the population is divided into groups, or clusters, and a random selection of these clusters is made. All elements within the selected clusters, or a random subset of them, are then included in the sample.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

Unlike stratified sampling, clusters are typically chosen so that each cluster is a miniature representation of the entire population, rather than being internally homogeneous. Cluster sampling is particularly useful when populations are geographically dispersed or when obtaining a complete sampling frame is difficult.

**Example 4**
In conducting a statewide voter preference poll, researchers may randomly select congressional districts and survey all voters, or a random selection of voters, within those districts. When clusters are chosen carefully, the resulting sample can provide a reliable picture of statewide voter preferences at a fraction of the cost of simple random sampling.

## Levels of Measurement (Types of Data)
Variables can be classified on the basis of their level of measurement. The way we classify variables greatly affects how we can use them in our analysis. Variables can be (1) nominal, (2) ordinal, (3) interval, or (4) ratio.

A nominal measurement is created when names are used to establish categories into which variables can be exclusively recorded. For example, sex can be classified as "male" or "female." You could also code it with a "1" or a "2"; oxide and carbonate ores may also be coded 1 and 2, but the numbers would serve only to indicate the categories and would carry no numerical significance; mathematical calculations using these codes would be meaningless. Soft drinks may be classified as Coke, Pepsi, 7-Up, or Ale 8. Each drink could be recorded in one of these categories to the exclusion of the others.

Nominal Measurements Names or classifications are used to divide data into separate and distinct categories.

It is important to remember that a nominal measurement carries no indication of order of preference, but merely establishes a categorical arrangement into which each observation can be placed.

Unlike a nominal measurement, an ordinal scale produces a distinct ordering or arrangement of the data. That is, the observations are ranked on the basis of some criterion. A retail company may rank its products as "good," "better," and "best." Opinion polls often use an ordinal scale such as "strongly agree," "agree," "no opinion," "disagree," and "strongly disagree."

As with nominal data, numbers can be used to order the rankings. Like nominal data, the magnitude of the numbers is not important; the ranking depends only on the order of the values. The retailer could have used the rankings of "1," "2," and "3," or "1," "3," and "12," for that matter. The arithmetic differences between the values are meaningless. A product ranked "2" is not twice as good as one with a ranking of "1."

Ordinal Measurements Measurements that rank observations into categories with a meaningful order.

Variables on an interval scale are measured numerically, and, like ordinal data, carry an inherent ranking or ordering. However, unlike the ordinal rankings, the differences between the values is important. Thus, the arithmetic operations of addition and subtraction are meaningful. The Fahrenheit scale for temperatures is an example of an interval scale. Not only is 70 degrees hotter than 60 degrees, but also the same difference of 10 degrees exists as between 90 and 100 degrees Fahrenheit.

The value of zero is arbitrarily chosen in an interval scale. There is nothing sacrosanct about the temperature of zero; it is merely an arbitrary reference point. The Fahrenheit scale could have been created so that zero was set at a much warmer (or colder) temperature. No specific meaning is attached to zero other than to say it is 10 degrees colder than 10 degrees Fahrenheit. Thus, 80 degrees is not twice as hot as 40 degrees and the ratio 80/40 has no meaning.

Interval Measurements Measurements on a numerical scale in which the value of zero is arbitrary but the difference between values is important.

Of all four levels of measurement, only the ratio scale is based on a numbering system in which zero is meaningful. Therefore, the arithmetic operations of multiplication and division also take on a rational interpretation. A ratio scale is used to measure many types of data found in business and geoscientific analyses. Variables such as costs, profits, inventory levels and grades are expressed as ratio measures. The value of zero dollars to measure revenues, for example, can be logically interpreted to mean that no sales have occurred. Furthermore, a firm with a 40 percent market share has twice as much of the market as a firm with a 20 percent market share. Measurements such as weight, time, and distance are also measured on a ratio scale since zero is meaningful, and an item that weighs 100 pounds is one-half as heavy as an item weighing 200 pounds.

Ratio Measurements Numerical measurements in which zero is a meaningful value and the difference between values is important.

You may notice that the four levels of measurement increase in sophistication, progressing from the crude nominal scale to the more refined ratio scale. Each measurement offers more information about the variable than did the previous one. This distinction among the various degrees of refinement is important, since different statistical techniques require different levels of measurements. While most statistical tests require interval or ratio measurements, other tests, called nonparametric tests (which will be examined later in this text), are designed to use nominal or ordinal data.

## Measures of Location and Dispersion
In addition to graphical summaries such as histograms and frequency curves, the information contained in a dataset can be summarized numerically. Two numerical characteristics are of primary importance: the \textbf{location} of the data and the \textbf{dispersion} of the observations.

Measures of location, also called \textbf{measures of central tendency}, describe a value that is typical or representative of the dataset. They indicate where the observations tend to cluster. Measures of dispersion describe how widely the observations are spread around this central value. A meaningful statistical description of data must consider both aspects together.

This section focuses on \textbf{measures of location}.

### Measures of Location
Different measures of location capture different ideas of what is meant by a ``typical'' value. The most commonly used measures of location are the mode, the mean, and the median.

\textbf{(a) The Mode}

The mode is the value that occurs most frequently in a dataset. A dataset with a single mode is unimodal\textbf{,} while a dataset with multiple modes is multimodal\textbf{.} The mode is especially useful for qualitative data and for identifying the most common outcome in a set of observations.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

**Example 1**
Consider the number of defects observed on identical manufactured components in a production batch:

2, 3, 1, 2, 0, 2, 1, 3, 2

The value 2 occurs most frequently. Therefore, the mode of the dataset is 2 defects per component. If two or more values occur with the same highest frequency, the dataset is multimodal, indicating that there is no single most typical value.

\textbf{(b) The Mean}

The mean is the most widely used measure of location. In general terms, the mean represents the ``balance point'' of the data. Several types of means are used in statistics, each appropriate for different types of data and applications.

\textbf{(i) Arithmetic Mean (AM)}

The arithmetic mean is defined as the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations. If a variable $x$takes values $x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}$, the arithmetic mean is denoted by $x$.

The arithmetic mean uses all observations in the dataset, but it is sensitive to extreme values\textbf{.}

For a set of observations $x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}$, the arithmetic mean is defined as:

\[
x=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}
\]

The arithmetic mean is the most widely used measure of location and incorporates all observations. However, it is sensitive to extreme values.

**Example 2**
Suppose the measured lengths (in mm) of five machined parts are:

98, 100, 101, 99, 102

The arithmetic mean length is:

\[
x=\frac{98+100+101+99+102}{5}=100 mm
\]

This value represents the average length of the parts. For large datasets, data are often grouped into classes. In such cases, the arithmetic mean is computed using class marks and frequencies, which provides an efficient approximation for large volumes of data.

\textbf{(ii) Geometric Mean}

The geometric mean is defined only for positive, non-zero values\textbf{.} It is obtained by taking the nth root of the product of the observations. The geometric mean is particularly appropriate when dealing with percentage changes, growth rates, and multiplicative processes\textbf{. }The geometric mean answers the question:

``What constant growth rate would produce the same overall change?''

The geometric mean is defined for positive, non-zero values and is given by:

$G=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}\right)^{1/n}$The geometric mean is appropriate for multiplicative processes, such as growth rates and percentage changes.

**Example 3 (Growth rate application)**
Suppose the annual growth rates of a company's output over three years are:

+10\%, +15\%, and +5\%

Expressed as growth factors, these are:

1.10, 1.15, and 1.05

The geometric mean growth factor is:

\[
G=\sqrt[3]{1.10\times 1.15\times 1.05}
\]

This value represents the average annual growth rate over the three years. Because growth compounds multiplicatively, the geometric mean provides a more meaningful summary than the arithmetic mean in such situations. It is always true that the geometric mean is less than or equal to the arithmetic mean, with equality only when all observations are equal.

\textbf{(iii) Harmonic Mean}

The harmonic mean is primarily used to compute average rates\textbf{,} particularly when a fixed quantity, such as distance or workload, is involved. It is defined as the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals of the observations. The harmonic mean is appropriate when averaging quantities expressed as ``per unit,'' such as speed or efficiency. The harmonic mean is used to compute average rates when a fixed quantity such as distance or workload is involved.

It is defined as:

\[
H=\frac{n}{\sum \frac{1}{x_{i}}}
\]

**Example 4**
An engineer drives a test vehicle over the same distance at two different speeds:\\
60 km/h on the first segment and 40 km/h on the second segment.

The average speed over the entire trip is not the arithmetic mean of 60 and 40. Instead, it is given by the harmonic mean:

\[
H=\frac{2}{\frac{1}{60}+\frac{1}{40}}=48 km/h
\]

This example illustrates why the harmonic mean is the correct measure when distances are fixed. For any set of positive numbers, the following inequality always holds:

\[
Harmonic \text{Mean}\le Geometric \text{Mean}\le Arithmetic \text{Mean}
\]

\textbf{(c) The Median}

The median is the middle value of an ordered dataset. If the number of observations is odd, the median is the central value. If the number of observations is even, the median is the average of the two central values. Unlike the mean, the median is resistant to extreme values\textbf{,} making it particularly useful for skewed data.

### Median for Ungrouped Data
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Median for Ungrouped Data}
Let $x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}$denote a random sample of size $n$, arranged in increasing order of magnitude.

If $n $is odd, the median is the observation in position

\[
\frac{n+1}{2}
\]

If $n $is even\textbf{,} the median is the average of the observations in positions

\[
\frac{n}{2}\ \text{and}\ \frac{n}{2}+1
\]

That is,

\[
\text{Median}=\left(\begin{matrix}x_{\frac{n+1}{2}}, & \text{if } n \text{ is odd}\\\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{\frac{n}{2}}+x_{\frac{n}{2}+1}\right), & \text{if } n \text{ is even}\end{matrix}\right)
\]

### Median for Grouped Data
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Median for Grouped Data}
When data are grouped into class intervals, the exact median cannot be determined directly. Instead, it is estimated using the median class, which is the class interval containing the median position.

The general formula for the median of grouped data is:

\[
\text{Median}=b_{L}+\left(\frac{\frac{n}{2}-f_{m-1}}{f_{m}}c\right)
\]

\emph{where:}

  - $b_{L}$= lower boundary of the median class
  - $n$= total number of observations
  - $f_{m-1}$= cumulative frequency of the class preceding the median class
  - $f_{m}$= frequency of the median class
  - $c$= class width

This formula assumes that observations are uniformly distributed within the median class.

**Example 5 (Ungrouped data)**
Consider the annual salaries (in thousands) of five engineers:

45, 48, 50, 52, 120

The arithmetic mean is inflated by the extremely high salary, while the median (50) better represents the typical salary in the group.

**Example 6 (Grouped data)**
Given:

  - $b_{L}=199.5$
  - $n=300$
  - $f_{m-1}=116$
  - $f_{m}=73$
  - $c=50$

Substituting into the formula:

\[
\text{Median}=199.5+\left(\frac{150-116}{73}\right)50
\]

\[
\text{Median}=199.5+(0.4658)(50)
\]

\[
\text{Median}\approx 222.8
\]

\textbf{(d) Relationship Between Measures of Location and Distribution Shape}

The relative positions of the mode, median, and mean depend on the shape of the frequency distribution. In symmetrical distributions, all three measures coincide. In positively skewed distributions, the mean is greater than the median, which is greater than the mode. In negatively skewed distributions, the mean is less than the median, which is less than the mode.

Understanding this relationship is essential for selecting an appropriate measure of location and for interpreting data correctly in engineering applications.

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

## Measure of Location and Dispersion
In addition to the histogram, the information in the frequency distribution can be further summarised by means of just two numbers. The first is the location of the data, and the various numbers that provide information about this are known as `measures of location' or `measures of central tendency'. `Location of the data' refers to a value that is typical of all the sample observations.

The second important aspect of the data is the dispersion of the observations. This implies how the data are scattered (dispersed). This is also called `measure of variation'.

## Measures of Dispersion
Measures of location describe the central or typical value of a dataset, but they do not reveal how the data are distributed around that central value. Two datasets may have the same mean or median yet differ greatly in their degree of variability. Measures of dispersion quantify this variability and describe how widely the observations are spread.

In engineering and scientific applications, dispersion is critically important. A process may achieve the correct average output, but if the variability is too high, the process may still be unreliable or unsafe. Measures of dispersion therefore provide insight into consistency, precision, stability, and risk\textbf{.}

Measures of dispersion can be broadly classified into three categories:

  - Measures based on extreme or selected values,
  - Measures based on deviations from a central value,
  - Measures based on squared deviations.

### Measures Based on Extreme or Selected Values
\textbf{(a) The Range}

The range is the simplest measure of dispersion and is defined as the difference between the largest and smallest observations in the dataset:

\[
\text{Range}=x_{\max}-x_{\min}
\]

**Example 1**
The tensile strengths (MPa) of five steel samples are:

410, 415, 420, 418, 412

\[
\text{Range}=420-410=10 MPa
\]

The range gives a quick indication of spread but depends only on two values and ignores the behavior of the remaining observations.

\textbf{(b) The Interquartile Range (IQR)}

The interquartile range (IQR) measures the spread of the central 50\% of the data and is defined as:

\[
\mathrm{IQR}=Q_{3}-Q_{1}
\]

where $Q_{1}$and $Q_{3}$are the first and third quartiles, respectively.

**Example 2**
If the 25th percentile of a dataset is 52 and the 75th percentile is 68:

\[
\mathrm{IQR}=68-52=16
\]

The IQR is resistant to extreme values and is commonly used in box plots and outlier detection.

\textbf{(c) The Interdecile Range}

The interdecile range measures the spread of the central 80\% of the data and is defined as:

\[
Interdecile \text{Range}=D_{9}-D_{1}
\]

This measure is mainly used in descriptive statistics because it is less convenient for theoretical analysis.

### Measures Based on Deviations from a Central Value
\textbf{(a) Mean Deviation}

The mean deviation is defined as the average of the deviations of observations from a central value (usually the mean or median)

\[
\text{Mean} Deviation=\frac{1}{n}\sum (x_{i}-x)
\]

Because positive and negative deviations cancel out, this measure can be misleading and is rarely used in practice.

\textbf{(b) Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)}

To avoid cancellation of deviations, the mean absolute deviation (MAD) uses absolute values:

\[
MAD=\frac{1}{n}\sum x_{i}-x
\]

**Example 3**
Consider the dataset:

6, 9, 10, 13, 17, 19, 21, 25

The absolute deviations from the mean are calculated and averaged to obtain the MAD.

The MAD is intuitive and uses all observations, but it is difficult to manipulate mathematically in advanced statistical analysis.

### Variance and Standard Deviation
To overcome the limitations of absolute deviations, statisticians square the deviations from the mean. This leads to the variance, which is the foundation of many statistical methods.

\textbf{(a) Variance}

For a sample, the variance is defined as:

\[
s^{2}=\frac{\sum (x_{i}-x)^{2}}{n-1}
\]

The denominator $n-1$is used to obtain an unbiased estimate of the population variance.

For a population, the variance is:

\[
\sigma ^{2}=\frac{\sum (x_{i}-\mu )^{2}}{n}
\]

\textbf{(b) Standard Deviation}

The standard deviation is the positive square root of the variance:

\[
s=\sqrt{s^{2}}
\]

The standard deviation is expressed in the same units as the original data, making it the most interpretable measure of dispersion.

### Types of Standard Deviation
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Types of Standard Deviation}
\textbf{(i) Population Standard Deviation}

Used when data include every member of the population:

\[
\sigma =\sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_{i}-\mu )^{2}}{n}}
\]

\textbf{(ii) Sample Standard Deviation}

Used when data represent a sample from a larger population:

\[
s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_{i}-x)^{2}}{n-1}}
\]

This is the most common form used in engineering and scientific analysis.

\textbf{(iii) Standard Deviation for Grouped Data}

When data are grouped into classes, the standard deviation is computed using class midpoints:

\[
s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum f_{i}(x_{i}^{'}-x)^{2}}{n-1}}
\]
where:

  - $x_{i}^{'}$is the class midpoint,
  - $f_{i}$is the class frequency,
  - $n$is the total number of observations.

**Example 4 (Sample Standard Deviation)**
Consider the measured voltages (V):

98, 100, 102, 100, 100

Mean:

\[
x=100
\]

Squared deviations:

\[
(98-100)^{2}=4,(100-100)^{2}=0,(102-100)^{2}=4
\]

\[
s^{2}=\frac{4+0+4+0+0}{4}=2
\]

\[
s=\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41 V
\]

This indicates that the voltages typically deviate from the mean by about 1.4 V.

### Interpretation of Standard Deviation
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Interpretation of Standard Deviation}

  - Small standard deviation $\rightarrow$ data tightly clustered $\rightarrow$ high precision
  - Large standard deviation $\rightarrow$ data widely spread $\rightarrow$ low consistency

In quality control and reliability engineering, reducing standard deviation is often more important than shifting the mean.

### Why Dispersion Matters in Engineering
Dispersion affects:

  - product quality,
  - system reliability,
  - safety margins,
  - risk of failure.

Two processes with identical means may perform very differently if their variability differs. Engineers must therefore evaluate dispersion before making decisions based on averages.

The modern theory of probability is generally traced to the mid-1600s, when mathematicians began analyzing games of chance using logical and numerical reasoning. A pivotal moment occurred through correspondence between \textbf{Blaise Pascal} and \textbf{Pierre de Fermat}.

They were asked to solve the ``problem of points,'' which concerned how to fairly divide the stakes of an unfinished gambling game. ## Determination of Probability of an Event
The probability of an event A, denoted, P(A), gives the numerical measure of the likelihood of the occurrence of event A which is such that 0 $\le$ P (A) $\le$ 1. If P (A) = 0, the event A is said to be impossible to occur and if P(A) = 1, A is said to be certain. If A\textsuperscript{/} is the complement of the event A, then P(A\textsuperscript{/}) = 1 -- P(A), called the probability that event A will not occur. There are three main schools of thought in defining and interpreting the probability of an event. These are the Classical Definition, Empirical Concept and the Subjective Approach. The first two are referred to as the Objective Approach.

  - \textbf{The Classical Definition:} This is based on the assumption that the outcomes of an experiment are equally likely. For example, if an experiment can lead to n mutually exclusive and equally likely outcomes, then the probability of the event A is defined by

\[
P(A)=\frac{n(A)}{n(S)}=\frac{\text{Number of successful outcomes}}{\text{Number of possible outcomes}}
\]

The classical definition of probability of event A is referred to as a priori probability because it is determined before any experiment is performed to observe the outcomes of event A.

  - \textbf{The Empirical Concept:} This concept uses the relative frequencies of past occurrences to develop probabilities for future. The probability of an event A happening in future is determined by observing what fraction of the time similar events happened in the past. That is,

\[
P(A)=\frac{\text{Number of times }A\text{ occurred in the past}}{\text{Total number of observations}}
\]

The relative frequency of the occurrence of the event A used to estimate P(A) becomes more accurate if trials are largely repeated. The relative frequency approach of defining P(A) is sometimes called posteriori probability because P(A) is determined only after event A is observed.

  - \textbf{The Subjective Definition:} The subjective concept of probability is based on the degree of belief through the evidence available. The probability of an event A may therefore be assessed through experience, intuitiveness, judgment or expertise. For example, determining the probability of getting a cure of a disease or going to rain today. This approach to probability has been developed relatively recently and is related to \emph{Bayesian Decision Analysis}. Although the subjective view of probability has enjoyed increased attention over the years, it has not been fully accepted by statisticians who have traditional orientations.

## Probability of Compound Events
Two or more events are combined to form a single event using the set operations, $\cup$ and $\cap$. The event

(i) (A $\cup$ B) occurs if either A or B both occur(s).

(ii) (A $\cap$ B) occurs if both A and B occur.

### Definitions:
(a) \textbf{Mutually Exclusive Events:} Two or more events which have no common outcome(s) (i.e. never occur at the same time) are said to be mutually exclusive. If A and B are mutually exclusive events of an experiment, then $A\cap B=\varnothing$ and $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$, since $P(A\cap B)=0$.

(b) \textbf{Independent Events:} Two or more events are said to be independent if the probability of occurrence of one is not influenced by the occurrence or non-occurrence of the other(s). Mathematically, the two events, A and B are said to be independent, if and only if $P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B)$. However, if A and B are such that, $P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B\mid A)$, they are said to be conditionally independent.

(c) \textbf{Conditional Probability}\textbf{:} Let A and B be two events in the sample space, S with P(B) > 0. The probability that an event A occurs given that event B has already occurred, denoted P(A/B), is called the conditional probability of A given B. The conditional probability of A given B is defined as.

\[
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0
\]

In particular, if S is a finite equiprobable space, then

\[
P(A\cap B)=\frac{n(A\cap B)}{n(S)},\; P(B)=\frac{n(B)}{n(S)}\ \text{and}\ P(A\mid B)=\frac{n(A\cap B)}{n(B)}
\]

(d). \textbf{Exhaustive Events:} Two or more events defined on the same sample space are said to be exhaustive if their union is equal to the sample space $S$ (thus, if they partition the sample space mutually exclusively). Eg: if $A_1,\;A_2,\;A_3\in S,$ $A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3=S$.

\textbf{Definition (partition of sample space): }The events $A_1,\;A_2,\;A_3,\;\dots,\;A_n$ form a partition of the same sample space $S$ if the following hold:

(a) $A_i\ne\varnothing$ for all $i=1,2,3,\dots,n$

(b) $A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing$ for all $i\ne j;\quad i,j=1,2,3,\dots,n$

(c)
\[
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_i=S
\]

In other words, the $n$- events $A_1,\;A_2,\;A_3,\;\dots,\;A_n$ form a partition of the sample space $S$ if the $n$- events are (a) nonempty, (b) mutually exclusive and (c) collectively exhaustive.

\textbf{The Multiplication Rule for }$P(A\cap B)$

The definition of conditional probability yields the following result, obtained by multiplying both sides of the conditional probability equation by \emph{P}(\emph{B}).

\begin{align*}
P(A\mid B)&=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\\
P(A\mid B)\times P(B)&=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\times P(B)\\
P(A\mid B)\times P(B)&=P(A\cap B)
\end{align*}

This rule is important because it is often the case that $P(A\cap B)$is desired, whereas both \emph{P}(\emph{B}) and $P(A\mid B)$can be specified from the problem description.

\textbf{The Law of Total Probability}

Let \emph{A}\textsubscript{1},..., \emph{A}\emph{\textsubscript{k}} be mutually exclusive and exhaustive events. Then for any other event \emph{B,}

\begin{align*}
P(B)&=P(B\mid A_1)\times P(A_1)+\dots+P(B\mid A_k)\times P(A_k)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(B\mid A_i)\times P(A_i)
\end{align*}

## Bayes' Rule
The power of Bayes' rule is that in many situations where we want to compute \emph{P(A|B)} it turns out that it is difficult to do so directly, yet we might have direct information about \emph{P(B|A)}. Bayes' rule enables us to compute \emph{P(A|B)} in terms of \emph{P(B|A).}

\[
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}
\]

### Bayes' Theorem
Let A and A\textsuperscript{c} constitute a partition of the sample space S such that with P(A) > 0 and P(A\textsuperscript{c}) > 0, then for any event B in S such that P(B) > 0,

\[
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A^{c})P(A^{c})}
\]

The denominator P(B) in the equation can be computed,

\begin{align*}
P(B)&=P\big[(A\cap B)\cup(A^{c}\cap B)\big]\\
&=P(A\cap B)+P(A^{c}\cap B)\\
&=P(A)P(B\mid A)+P(A^{c})P(B\mid A^{c})\qquad\text{(by multiplicative rule)}
\end{align*}

## Axioms of Probability
Given an experiment and a sample space, S, the objective of probability is to assign to each event A a number P(A), called the probability of the event A, which will give a precise measure of the chance that A will occur. To ensure that the probability assignments will be consistent with our intuitive notions of probability, all assignments should satisfy the following axioms (basic properties) of probability.

\textbf{A.1:} For every event A, 0 $\le$ P(A) $\le$1

\[
A.2: P(S) = 1
\]

\textbf{A.3:} If A and B are mutually exclusive events, i.e $A\cap B=\varnothing$ then

$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$.

\textbf{A.4:} If $A_1,\;A_2,\;A_3,\;\dots,\;A_n$ is a sequence of $n$ mutually exclusive events, then,

$P(A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3\cup\dots\cup A_n)=P(A_1)+P(A_2)+P(A_3)+\dots+P(A_n)$ or

(
\[
P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(A_i)
\]
.

The following theorems arise directly from the above axioms:

(i) \textbf{Theorem 1:} If $\varnothing$ is the empty set, then $P(\varnothing)=0$.

\emph{Proof:}

Let A be any event, then A and $\varnothing$ are mutually exclusive and A = A $\cup$$\varnothing$

Then by A.3 $P(A)=P(A\cup\varnothing)=P(A)+P(\varnothing)$ and $P(\varnothing)=0$

(ii) \textbf{Theorem 2:} If $A^{c}$ is the complement of an event $A$, then

\[
P(A^{c})=1-P(A)
\]

\emph{Proof}

\[
S=A\cup A^{c}
\]

\[
P(S)=P(A)+P(A^{c})
\]

$1=P(A)+P(A^{c})$, by A.2

\[
P(A^{c})=1-P(A)
\]

### Some Rules of Probability
(a) \textbf{The Addition Rule:}

Let $A_1,\;A_2,\;A_3,\;\dots,\;A_n$ be events of the sample space, S. Then

(i) $P(A_1\cup A_2)=P(A_1)+P(A_2)-P(A_1\cap A_2)$

(ii)$P(A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3)=P(A_1)+P(A_2)+P(A_3)-P(A_1\cap A_2)$$-P(A_1\cap A_3)-P(A_2\cap A_3)$+

$P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3)$.

If the events $A_1,\;A_2,\;A_3,\;\dots,\;A_n$ are mutually exclusive, then

(i) $P(A_1\cup A_2)=P(A_1)+P(A_2)$

(ii) $P(A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3)=P(A_1)+P(A_2)+P(A_3)$

(iii) $P(A_1\cup A_2\cup\dots\cup A_n)=P(A_1)+P(A_2)+P(A_3)+\dots+P(A_n)$

(b) \textbf{The Multiplication Theorem:}

If $A_1,\;A_2,\;A_3,\;\dots,\;A_n$ are events of the same sample space, S, then

(i) $P(A_1\cap A_2)=P(A_1)\times P(A_2\mid A_1)$

(ii) $P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3)=P(A_1)\times P(A_2\mid A_1)\times P(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2)$

## Application of Counting Techniques
The classical definition of probability of an event $A,\;P(A)$ requires the knowledge of the number of outcomes of $A$ and the total possible outcomes of the experiment, $S$. To find these outcomes we list such outcomes explicitly, which may be impossible if they are too many. Counting Techniques may be useful to determine the number of outcomes and compute $P(A)$. We shall examine three basic counting techniques, namely the \emph{Multiplication Principle, Permutation} and \emph{Combination}

### The Multiplication Principle
The \emph{Multiplication Principle}, also known as the \emph{Basic Counting Principle} states that:

  - If an operation can be performed in $n_1$ ways, and a second operation can be performed in $n_2$ ways and so on for $k\text{th}$ operation which can be performed in $n_k$ ways, then the combined experiment or operations can be performed in $n_1\times n_2\times n_3\times\dots\times n_k$ ways.

\emph{For example}: A homeowner doing some remodeling requires the services of both a plumbing contractor and an electrical contractor. If there are 12 plumbing contractors and 9 electrical contractors available in the area, in how many ways can the contractors be chosen? If we denote the plumbers by \emph{P}\textsubscript{1},..., \emph{P}\textsubscript{12} and the electricians by \emph{Q}\textsubscript{1},..., \emph{Q}\textsubscript{9}, then we wish the number of pairs of the form (P\textsubscript{i},Q\textsubscript{j}). With n\textsubscript{1}=12 and n\textsubscript{2}=9, the product rule yields N = (12)(9) = 108 possible ways of choosing the two types of contractors.

### Permutation of Objects
An ordered arrangement of objects is called a permutation. For example, the possible permutations of the letters $A,\;B$ and $C$ are as follows: $ABC,\;ACB,\;BAC,\;BCA$, $CAB,\;CBA$.

\textbf{Definitions:}

\[
n!=n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)\dots 3\times 2\times 1\quad(\text{or }{}^{n}P_{n})
\]

  - The number of permutations of $n$ distinct objects taken $k$ at a time is:

\[
{}^{n}P_k\ \text{or}\ P(n,k)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!},\ \text{where }k<n
\]

  - The number of permutations of $n$ objects consisting of groups of which $n_1$ of the first group are alike, $n_2$ of the second group are alike and so on for the $k\text{th}$ group with $n_k$ objects which are alike is:

\[
\frac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2!\,n_3!\dots n_k!}
\]
 where
\[
n_1+n_2+n_3+\dots+n_k=n
\]

  - \emph{Circular Permutations: }Permutations that occur when objects are arranged in a circle are called circular permutations. The number of ways of arranging $n$ different objects in a circle is given by:

\[
\frac{n!}{n}=(n-1)!
\]

## Random Variables and Distribution

## Introduction
Experiments are conducted with results that are subject to chance. Suppose that to each point of a sample space we assign a number, we then have a function defined on the sample space. This function is called a random variable (or stochastic variable). In other words, in a particular experiment a random variable X would be some function that assigns a real number X (S) to each possible outcome $s\in S$.

\textbf{Definition: }Let $S$ be the sample space associated with some experiment $\varepsilon$. A random variable $X$ is a function that assigns a real number $X(s)$ to each sample point $s\in S$.

There are two basic types of random variables: the Discrete random variable and the Continuous random variable.

## Discrete Random Variable
A \textbf{discrete }random variable is a random variable whose possible values either constitute a finite set or else can be listed in an infinite sequence in which there is a first element, a second element, and so on (``countably'' infinite).

### Probability Distribution of Discrete Random Variable
For a random variable $X$ of the discrete type, it is necessary and sufficient to know the following:

  - the list of all possible values $x_i,\; i=1,2,3,\dots$
  - the corresponding probability $p(X=x_i)$ of each of these values.

When such information is available we say that the distribution law or the probability distribution or simply the distribution of the random variable $X$ is known.

\emph{\textbf{Definition}}\textbf{: }A probability distribution of a discrete random variable $X$ is a sequence of the

values of $X$, together with the probability assigned to each point $x_i,\;(i=1,2,3,\dots)$$x_i,\ i=1,2,3,\dots$.

The probability distribution of a discrete random variable is more often called probability function $(p.f.)$ or a probability mass function $(p.m.f.)$ and is frequently denoted by $p(x_i)$ and $P(X=x_i)$.

\emph{\textbf{Representation of Probability Distribution of the Discrete Random Variable}}

The probability distribution of a discrete random variable $X$ can be represented by a table, a graph or a function.

\emph{\textbf{Tabular form}}

It is convenient to specify the probability distribution of a random variable by means of a table having two rows: the upper row contains the possible values the random variable assumes and the lower row contains the corresponding probabilities of the values. \emph{\textbf{Graphical form}}

The probability distribution may also be given graphically. The graph represents chance and not data.

\textbf{Definition: }A graph of $p(x_i)$ against $x_i$ is called a probability graph.

To obtain a probability graph, vertical lines or bars are drawn above the possible values $x_i$ of the random variable $X$ on the horizontal axis. The height of each line or bar (rectangle) equals the probabilities of the corresponding values of the random variables.

\textbf{Formula:}

Representing a discrete probability by means of a formula is the most useful method.

For example:

\[
p(x)=P(X=x)=\frac{1}{n},\qquad x=1,2,3,\dots,n
\]

defines a probability distribution of a random variable $X$. Not all functions of a discrete random variable qualify to be called probability mass function. We shall now give a formal definition of a probability mass function.

\emph{\textbf{Definition:}}\textbf{ Probability mass function: }Any function $p(x)$ defined on all possible values of the discrete random variable $X=x_i,\ i=1,2,3,\dots$ is called a probability mass function if it satisfies the following properties:

\begin{align*}
&\text{property 1}\qquad p(x_i)\ge 0,\quad i=1,2,3,\dots\\
&\text{property 2}\qquad \sum_i p(x_i)=1
\end{align*}

where the summation is over all possible values of the random variable $X$.

A random variable $X$in one experiment takes only one of its $n$ possible values with a corresponding probability which is non-negative. Thus, in an experiment, one and only one of the numerous possible mutually exclusive events will happen. Consequently, the sum of the probabilities of these events:

\[
p(x_1)+p(x_2)+p(x_3)+p(x_4)+\dots+p(x_n)=1
\]

## Continuous Random Variable
If $X$ is a continuous random variable, the probability that $X$ takes on any one particular value is generally zero. Therefore, we cannot define a continuous random variable in the same way as for a discrete random variable. In order to arrive at a probability distribution for a continuous random variable we note that the probability that $X$ lies between two different values is meaningful. Thus, a continuous random variable is the type whose spaces are not composed of a countable number of points but takes on values in some interval or a union of intervals of the real line.

\textbf{Definition:} If the set of all possible values of a random variable $X$, takes on an uncountably infinite number of values or values in some interval or a union of intervals of the real line, it is called a continuous random variable if there exists a function $f$, called probability density function of $X$ such that

(i) $f(x_i)\ge 0$ $\forall x_i$ (Non-negative)

(ii)
\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x_i)\,dx=1
\]

(iii)
\[
P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\qquad -\infty\le a\le b\le\infty
\]

### Probability Distribution of Continuous Random Variable
Continuous random variable can be considered as one that takes on values in some interval of the real line. The probability distribution for a continuous random variable is more often called a probability density function (p.d.f) or simply density function and is denoted by $f(x)$.

\textbf{Definition:} The probability density function, (p.d.f.) of a random variable $X$ of the continuous type, with space R that is an interval or union of intervals, defined on the set of real numbers is an integrable function $f(x)$ satisfying the following conditions.

(i) $f(x_i)\ge 0$ $\forall x_i$ (Non-negative)

(ii)
\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x_i)\,dx=1
\]

(iii) The probability of the event X $\in$ A is
\[
P(X\in A)=\int_A f(x)\,dx
\]

## Cumulative Distribution Function
The cumulative distribution function (c.d.f.) or simply the distribution function is the most universal characteristic of a random variable. It exists for all random variables whether they are discrete or continuous.

\textbf{Definition: }Let $X$ be a random variable and $x$ any real number. The cumulative distribution function of $X$ is a function $F(x)$ defined as the probability that the random variable $X$ takes a value less than or equal to $x$ such that

\[
F(x)=P(X\le x)
\]

The function $F(x)$ in the above definition may also be written as

\[
F(x)=P(-\infty<X\le x)
\]

### Cumulative distribution function of Discrete Random Variables
The statement of the theorem below for a distribution function of a discrete random variable follows trivially from the definition of cumulative distribution function.

\textbf{Theorem (Distribution function of a discrete random variable): }Let $X$ be a discrete random variable with probability $p(x_i)$, then the cumulative distribution function is defined by

\[
F(x)=\sum_{x_i\le x}p(x_i)
\]

For the discrete case, the random variable $X$ takes on only a finite number of values $x_1,\;x_2,\;x_3,\;\dots,\;x_n$. In this case, the distribution function $F(x)$ is given by

\[
F(x)=\begin{cases}0 & -\infty\le x<x_1\\ p(x_1) & x_1\le x<x_2\\ p(x_1)+p(x_2) & x_2\le x<x_3\\ \vdots & \vdots\\ p(x_1)+p(x_2)+\dots+p(x_n) & x_n\le x<+\infty\end{cases}
\]

The figure below depicts the graph $F(x)$ which is discontinuous at at the possible values $x_1,\;x_2,\;x_3,\;\dots,\;x_n$. At these points $F(x)$ is continuous from the right but discontinuous from the left. Because of the appearance of its graph, the cumulative distribution function for a discrete random variable is also called a staircase function or a step function, having jump discontinuities at all possible values $X=x_i$ with a step at the $x_i$ of height $p(x_i)$. The graph increases only through these jumps at $x_1,\;x_2,\;x_3,\;\dots,\;x_n$. Everywhere else in the interval $[x_i,\;x_{i+1}]$, the cumulative distribution function $F(x_i)$ is constant.

## Special Probability Distributions

## Introduction
In general, it is useful to present the probability distribution of a random variable by a model. Probability calculations can then be made convenient by substituting appropriate values into the algebraic model.

In this section, we shall \emph{\textbf{briefly}} define certain special distributions that are widely used in applications of probability and statistics. Distributions that would be considered include both discrete and continuous univariate distributions.

## Discrete Probability Distribution

### Bernoulli Distribution:
A single trial of an experiment may result in one of the two mutually exclusive outcomes such as defective and non-defective, dead or alive, yes or no, male or female, etc. Such a trial is called $\text{Bernoulli trial}$ and a sequence of these trials form a $\text{Bernoulli}$process, satisfying the following conditions:

(i). Each trial results in one of the two mutually exclusive outcomes, \emph{success} and \emph{failure. }

(ii). The probability of a success, $p$ remains constant, from trial to trial. The probability

of failure is denoted by $q=1-p$.

(iii). The trials are independent. That is, the outcome of any particular trial is not

affected by the outcome of any other trial.

\textbf{Definition: }

A random variable, is said to have a Bernoulli distribution if it assumes the values 0 and 1 for the two outcomes. The probability distribution for the success in the trial is defined by

$P(x)=p^{x}(1-p)^{1-x},\quad x=0\ \text{or}\ 1$ and $0<p<1$

where the mean and variance of the distribution are as follows:

\begin{align*}\mu&=E(X)=p;\\ \sigma^2&=\Var(X)=p(1-p)\end{align*}

An important distribution arising from counting the number of successes in a fixed number of independent Bernoulli trials is the Binomial distribution.

### The Binomial Distribution
The binomial distribution is a discrete probability distribution, where the experiment is repeated \emph{n} times under identical conditions and each of the \emph{n }trials is independent of each other which results in one of the two outcomes.

Thus, in the event of independent trials (often called Bernoulli trials) let $p$ be the probability that an event will happen (success) and $q=1-p$ the probability that the event will fail in any single trial. Such experiments are called Binomial experiments and the probability that the event will happen exactly $x$ times in $n$ trials is given by the probability function:

\[
f(x)=\Pr(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^{x}q^{n-x}=\frac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!}\,p^{x}q^{n-x}
\]
.

where the random variable denotes the number of success in $n$ trials and $x=0,1,2,3,\dots,n$.

The shape of the distribution depends on the two parameters $n$ and $p$.

  - when $p<0.5$ and $n$ is small, the distribution will be skewed to the right.
  - when $p>0.5$ and $n$ is small, the distribution will be skewed to the left
  - when $p=0.5$ the distribution will be symmetric.
  - In all cases, as $n$ gets larger the distribution gets closer to being a symmetric, bell-shaped distribution.

\textbf{Properties}

Mean $\mu=np$

Variance $\sigma^2=npq$

Standard Deviation $\sigma$ = $\sqrt{npq}$

### Negative Binomial Distribution (Pascal's Distribution)
Let us consider an experiment in which the properties are the same as those listed for a binomial experiment with the exception that the trials will be repeated until a fixed number of successes occur. Therefore, instead of finding the probability of x successes in n trials, where n is fixed, we are now interested in the probability that the\dots{}k\textsuperscript{th}success occurs on the x\textsuperscript{th}trial. Experiments of this kind are called `negative binomial experiments'. (Walpole and Myres, 1993). The number X of trials to produce k successes in a negative binomial experiment is called a ``negative binomial random variable'' and its probability distribution is called the ``negative binomial distribution''.

Since its probabilities depend on the number of successes desired and the probability of success on a given trial, we shall denote them by the symbol b\textsuperscript{*}(x;k,p ). For the general formula b\textsuperscript{*}(x; k, p), consider the probability of a success on the trial preceded by k-1 successes and x-k failures in some specified order. The probability for the specified order ending in success is p\textsuperscript{k-1}q\textsuperscript{x-k}p = p\textsuperscript{k}q\textsuperscript{x-k}. The total number of sample points in the experiment ending in success, after the occurrence of k-1 successes and x-k failures in any order is equal to the number of partitions of x-1 trials into two groups with k-1 successes corresponding to one group and x-k failures corresponding to the other group. This number is given by the term $\binom{x-1}{k-1}$, each mutually exclusive and occurring with equal probability p\textsuperscript{k}q\textsuperscript{x-k}. We obtain the general formula by multiplying p\textsuperscript{k}q\textsuperscript{x-k} by $\binom{x-1}{k-1}$.

In other words:

\[
b^{\ast}(x;k,p)=\binom{x-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}q^{x-k}p\;=\;\binom{x-1}{k-1}p^{k}q^{x-k}\qquad x=k,k+1,\dots
\]

p = probability of success

q = (1 - p) = probability of failure

x = total number of trials on which the k\textsuperscript{th} success occurs.

\textbf{Areas of application }of negative binomial distribution include many biological situations such as death of insects, number of insect bites per fruit (e.g. mango).

### Poisson Distribution
\textbf{Introduction:}

Experiments yielding numerical values of a random variable (x), the number of successes occuring during a given time interval or in a specified region, are often called poisson experiments. The given time interval may be of any length, such as a minute, a day, a week, a month or even a year. Hence, a poisson experiment might generate observations for the random variable representing the number of telephone calls per hour received by an office, the number of days school is closed due to snow during the winter, or the number of postponed games due to rain during a basketball season. The specified region could be a line segment, an area, a volume or perhaps a material. In this case, might represent the number of field mice per acre, the number of bacteria in a given culture, or the number of typing errors per page.

\emph{\textbf{The Poisson process:}}

A poisson experiment is derived from the Poisson process and possesses the following properties:

  - The number of successes occuring in one time interval or specified region are independent of those occurring in any other disjoint time interval or region of space.
  - The probability of a single success occuring during a very short time interval or in a small region is proportional to the length of the time interval or the size of the region and does not depend on the number of successes occuring outside this time interval or region.
  - The probability of more than one success occuring in such a short time interval or falling in such a small region is negligible.

The probability distribution of the Poisson random variable is called the Poisson distribution and is denoted by $P(x;\mu)$ since its values depend only on $\mu$, the average number of successes occuring in the given time interval or specified region. This formula is given by the definition below:

\textbf{Definition: }The probability distribution of the Poisson random variable, representing the number of successes occuring in a given time interval or specified region is given by:

\[
P(x;\mu)=\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{x}}{x!};\qquad x=0,1,2,3,\dots,
\]

Where $\mu$ is the average number of successes occuring in the given time interval or specified region and $e=2.71828$.

\textbf{Theorem:} The mean and variance of the Poisson distribution $P(x;\mu)$ both have the value $\lambda$.

### Relationship between the Binomial and Poisson distributions
In the binomial distribution, if n is large while the probability p of occurrence of an event is close to zero, so that q = 1 -- p is close to one, the event is called a \textbf{`rare event'}. In practice, we shall consider an event as rare if the number of trials is at least 50 (n > 50) while np is less than 5. For such cases the Poisson distribution very closely approximates the binomial distribution $f(x)=\frac{\lambda^{x}e^{-\lambda}}{x!}$. with $\lambda$ = np (Spiegel, 1980). Thus the Poisson distribution is a limiting case of the Binomial distribution when n$\rightarrow$$\infty$, p$\rightarrow$0 and np (n = $\mu$) remain constant (Davis, 1986).

## Continuous Probability Distribution

### The Normal Distribution:
The graph of the normal distribution which is a bell-shaped smooth curve approximately describes many phenomena that occur in nature, industry and research. In addition, errors in scientific measurements are extremely well approximated by a normal distribution. Thus, the normal distribution is one of the most widely used probability distributions for modelling random experiments. It provides a good model for continuous random variables involving measurements such as time, heights/weights of persons, marks scored in an examination, amount of rainfall, growth rate and many other scientific measurements.

\textbf{Definition:}

The probability density function for the normal random variable X which is simply called normal distribution is defined by:

\[
f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}}, & -\infty<x<\infty\\[6pt] 0, & \text{elsewhere}\end{cases}
\]

Where $\sigma>0,\ -\infty<x<\infty$ and the mean and variance of the measurements $x$, are $E(x)=\mu$ and $\Var(x)=\sigma^{2}$.

If the random variable is modelled by the normal distribution with mean, $\mu$ and variance, $\sigma^{2}$ then it is simply denoted as $x\sim N(\mu,\sigma)$.

This fact considerably simplifies the calculations of probabilities concerning normally distributed variables, as seen in the following illustration:

Suppose that X is$N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$, let c\textsubscript{1}<c\textsubscript{2}, and since$P(X=c_1)=0$, then

\begin{align*}
P(c_1<X<c_2) &=P(X<c_2)-P(X<c_1)\\
&=P\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}<\frac{c_2-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-P\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}<\frac{c_1-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\\
&=\Phi\left(\frac{c_2-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{c_1-\mu}{\sigma}\right)
\end{align*}

Note that$\Phi(-x)=1-\Phi(x)$.

The normal distribution possesses the following properties.

\textbf{Properties}

Mean $E(X)=\mu$

Variance $\Var(X)=\sigma^{2}$

Standard Deviation$=\sigma$

### Normal approximation to the Binomial
Whenever the number of trials in a binomial experiment is small it is easy to find the probabilities associated with the experiment quickly as calculations become laborious. The normal distribution is often a good approximation to a discrete distribution when the latter takes on a symmetrical bell shape.

If X is a binomial random variable with mean $\mu$ = \emph{np }and variance $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2}. = \emph{npq}, then the limiting form of the distribution of:

\[
Z=\frac{X-np}{\sqrt{npq}}\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty
\]

is the standard normal distribution n(Z;0,1).

It turns out that the normal distribution with $\mu$ = \emph{np} and $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2} = \emph{npq} = \emph{np(1- p)} not only provides a very accurate approximation to the binomial distribution when \emph{n} is large and \emph{p} is not extremely close to 0 or 1, but also provides a fairly good approximation even when \emph{n} is small and \emph{p} is reasonably close to.

In fact,
\[
\frac{X-np}{\sqrt{npq}}
\]
 is asymptotically normal.

### The Gamma Distribution:
The Gamma distribution arises in the study of waiting times, for example, in the lifetime of devices. It is also useful in modeling many nonnegative continuous variables. The gamma distribution requires the knowledge of the gamma function.

\textbf{Definition:} The gamma function is defined by

\[
\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}\,dx
\]
 for
\[
\alpha>0
\]
 and for all \emph{x > 0}

$\Gamma(\alpha)$ is read as ``gamma function of $\alpha$

Integrating by parts with $u=x^{\alpha-1}$ and $dv=e^{-x}\,dx$, we obtain

\begin{align*}
\Gamma(\alpha) &=-e^{-x}x^{\alpha-1}\Big|_0^{\infty}+\int_0^{\infty}(\alpha-1)x^{\alpha-2}e^{-x}\,dx\\
&=(\alpha-1)\int_0^{\infty}x^{\alpha-2}e^{-x}\,dx
\end{align*}

Which yields the recursion formula

$\Gamma(\alpha)=(\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha-1)$.

Repeated application of the recursion formula gives

\begin{align*}
\Gamma(\alpha) &=(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)\Gamma(\alpha-2)\\
&=(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)(\alpha-3)\Gamma(\alpha-3)
\end{align*}

and so forth. Note that when $\alpha=n$, where n is a positive integer,

$\Gamma(n)=(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)\dots\Gamma(1)$=$(n-1)$$!$

\emph{\textbf{General Gamma Distribution:}}

The continuous random variable has a gamma distribution, with parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ if its density function is given by:

\[
f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\beta^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\frac{x}{\beta}} & x>0\\[6pt] 0 & \text{elsewhere}\end{cases}
\]

where $\alpha>0$ and $\beta>0$.

\textbf{Property of Gamma distribution}

  - \textbf{E(X) = }$\alpha\beta$
  - \textbf{Var(X) = }$\alpha\beta^{2}$

### Exponential Distribution
An exponential distribution is a special gamma distribution for which $\alpha=1$. It has many applications in the field of statistics, particularly in the areas of reliability theory and waiting times or queueing problems. It is a continuous distribution that can be related to the Poisson distribution in the discrete sense.

\textbf{Definition: }A continuous random variable has an exponential distribution with parameter $\beta$ if its density function is given by:

\[
f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\beta}e^{-\frac{x}{\beta}} & x>0\\[6pt] 0 & \text{elsewhere}\end{cases}
\]

where $\beta>0$.

\textbf{Properties}

Mean $E(X)=\theta$

Variance $\Var(X)=\theta^{2}$

Standard Deviation $\sigma=\theta$

So if $\lambda$is the mean of changes in the unit interval, then $\theta=\frac{1}{\lambda}$is the mean waiting time for the first change.

### Covariance
When we consider the joint distribution of two random variables, the mean, the median and the variance provide useful information about their marginal distribution. However, these values do not provide any information about the relationship between the two variables or about their tendency to vary together rather than independently. The quantity covariance enables us to measure the association between two random variables, to determine the variance of the sum of any number of dependent random variables and to predict the value of one random variable by using the observed value of some other related variable.

Let X and Y be random variables having a specified joint distribution and Let E(x)=$\mu$\textsubscript{x}, E(Y) = $\mu$\textsubscript{y}, Var(X)= $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{x} and Var (Y)= $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{y}. The covariance of X and Y, (denoted by COV (X,Y), is defined as:

Cov(X,Y) = $\sigma$\textsubscript{xy}= E[(X-$\mu$\textsubscript{x})(Y-$\mu$\textsubscript{y})]

=$\sum$$\sum$(X-$\mu$\textsubscript{x})(Y -$\mu$\textsubscript{y})]f(x,y) if X and Y are discrete, and

\[
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-\mu_x)(y-\mu_y)f(xy)\,dx\,dy\qquad\text{if }X\text{ and }Y\text{ are continuous.}
\]

It can be shown (DeGroot, 1989) that if $\sigma$\textsubscript{x}\textsuperscript{2}< $\infty$ and $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{y}<$\infty$, then the expectation above will exist and cov(x,y) can be finite. However, the value of cov(x,y) can be positive, negative or zero. When X and Y are independent covariance is zero. The converse however, is not generally true. Two variables may have zero covariance and still not be statistically independent. To dependent variables can be unrelated.

That $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{y}<$\infty$ and $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2}\textsubscript{y}<$\infty$,

# Chapter 6: Introduction to Estimation
Learning objectives:

  - Understand point and interval estimates and how it can be applied to engineering data.

## Estimation

## Introduction
The basic reasons for the need to estimate population parameters from sample information is that it is ordinarily too expensive or simply infeasible to enumerate complete populations to obtain the required information. The cost of complete censuses may be prohibitive in finite populations while complete enumerations are impossible in the case of infinite populations. Hence, estimation procedures are useful in providing the means of obtaining estimates of population parameters with desired degree of precision. We now consider \emph{estimation,} the first of the two general areas of \emph{statistical inference.} The second general area is \emph{hypothesis testing} which will be examined later.

The subject of \emph{estimation} is concerned with the methods by which population characteristics are measured from sample information. The objectives are to present:

  - properties for judging how well a given sample statistic estimates the parent population parameter.
  - several methods for estimating these parameters.

There are basically two types of estimation: \emph{point estimation }and \emph{interval estimation.} In \emph{point estimation}, a single sample statistic, such as$\overline{X}$,$s$or$\overline{p}$is calculated from the sample to provide a best estimate of the true value of the corresponding population parameter such as$\mu,$$\sigma$or $p$. Such a statistic is termed a point\emph{ estimator.}

The function or rule that is used to estimate the value of a parameter is called an \emph{estimator.} An \emph{estimate} is a particular value calculated from a particular sample of observations.

On the other hand, an \emph{interval estimate }consists of two numerical values defining an interval which, with varying degrees of confidence, we feel includes the parameter being estimated.

## Properties of a Point Estimator
\textbf{U}\emph{\textbf{nbiasedness}}\emph{:} If the expected value or mean of all possible values of a statistic over all possible samples is equal to the population parameter being estimated, the sample statistic is said to be unbiased. That is, if the expected value of an estimator is equal to the corresponding population parameter, the estimator is unbiased.

**Example 66**
The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean.

\[
E(\overline{X})=E\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\right]=\mu
\]

\emph{\textbf{Efficiency:}} The most efficient estimator among a group of unbiased estimators is the one with the smallest variance. This concept refers to the sampling variability of an estimator.

**Example 67**
Let$\hat{\theta}_1$,$\hat{\theta}_2$and$\hat{\theta}_3$be unbiased estimators for$\theta$. Let$\hat{\theta}_4$be a biased estimator among this group of estimators. Assume that$\Var(\hat{\theta}_4)<\Var(\hat{\theta}_2)<\Var(\hat{\theta}_1)<\Var(\hat{\theta}_3)$Which of these estimators is most efficient?

\emph{\textbf{Consistency:}} An estimator is consistent if as the sample size increases, the probability increases that the estimator will approach the true value of the population parameter. Alternatively, an estimator is consistent if it satisfies the following conditions:

  - $\Var(\hat{\theta})\to0$as$n\to\infty$
  - $\hat{\theta}$ becomes unbiased as$n\to\infty$

## Interval Estimation
For most practical purposes, it would not suffice to have merely a single value estimate of a population parameter. Any single point estimate will be either right or wrong. Therefore, instead of obtaining only a single estimate of a population parameter, it would certainly seem to extremely useful and perhaps necessary to obtain two estimators, say$\overline{X}_1$and$\overline{X}_2$, and say with some confidence that the interval between$\overline{X}_1$and$\overline{X}_2$includes the true mean$\mu$.

Thus, an interval estimate of a population parameter is a statement of two values between which it is estimated that the parameter lies.

We shall be discussing the construction of confidence intervals as a means of interval estimation.

The confidence we have that a population parameter,$\theta$, will fall within some confidence interval will equal$(1-\alpha)$, where$\alpha$is the probability that the interval does not contain$\theta$(i.e. the probability$\alpha$, is an allowance for error).

To construct a 95\% confidence interval$\Rightarrow$$\alpha=0.05$. That is, the probability is 0.05 that the value$\theta$will not lie within the interval.

Note that

\[
\alpha+(\text{Confidence limit level})=1.0
\]

The larger the confidence interval, the smaller the probability of error$(\alpha)$ for the interval estimator.

\emph{Confidence Interval For}$\mu$$(\sigma\ \text{known})$

A confidence interval is constructed on the basis of sample information. It also depends on the size of$\alpha$. Assume the population variance$\sigma^{2}$is known and the population is normal, then the$100(1-\alpha)\%$percent C.I for$\mu$is given by

\[
\overline{X}-Z_{\alpha/2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\le\mu\le\overline{X}+Z_{\alpha/2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
\]

Simply written as

\[
\overline{X}\pm Z_{\alpha/2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
\]

where$Z_{\alpha/2}$ is the Z value representing an area $\alpha/2$to the right and left tails of the standard normal probability distribution.

## Confidence Interval For A Population Proportion
It is often necessary to construct confidence intervals on a population proportion. For example, suppose that a random sample of size \emph{n }has been taken from a large (possibly infinite) population and that \emph{X} ($\le$ \emph{n}) observations in this sample belong to a class of interest. Then $\overline{P}=X/n$ is a point estimator of the proportion of the population \emph{p }that belongs to this class. Note that \emph{n }and \emph{p }are the parameters of a binomial distribution. Furthermore, we know that the sampling distribution of $\overline{P}$ is approximately normal with mean \emph{p }and variance$p(1-p)/n$ if \emph{p }is not too close to either 0 or 1 and if \emph{n }is relatively large. Typically, to apply this approximation we require that$np$and $n(1-p)$be greater than or equal to 5. We will make use of the normal approximation in this regard.

\textbf{Definition}: If \emph{n }is large, the distribution of

\[
Z=\frac{X-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}=\frac{\overline{P}-p}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}}
\]

is approximately standard normal.

The$100(1-\alpha)\%$CI for \emph{p} is then given by

\[
\overline{P}-Z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\le p\le\overline{P}+Z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
\]

Note that the quantity$\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ in the equation above is called the \textbf{standard error of the point estimator}$\overline{P}$\textbf{. }Unfortunately, the upper and lower limits of the confidence interval obtained from this equation contain the unknown parameter \emph{p}. However, a satisfactory solution is to replace \emph{p }by$\overline{P}$ in the standard error, which results in

\[
\overline{P}-Z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\overline{P}(1-\overline{P})}{n}}\le p\le\overline{P}+Z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\overline{P}(1-\overline{P})}{n}}
\]

This procedure depends on the adequacy of the normal approximation to the binomial. To be reasonably conservative, this requires that $np$and $n(1-p)$be greater than or equal to 5. In situations where this approximation is inappropriate, particularly in cases where \emph{n }is small, other methods must be used.

## Tests of Hypotheses and Significance

## Introduction
We now discuss the subject of hypothesis testing, which as earlier noted is one of the two basic classes of statistical inference. Testing of hypotheses involves using statistical inference to test the validity of postulated values for population parameters. If the hypothesis specifies the distribution completely it is called simple, otherwise it is called composite. For example, a demographer interested in the mean age of residents in a certain local government area might pose a simple hypothesis such as$\mu=24$or he might specify a composite hypothesis as$\mu<24$or$\mu>24$.

A statistical test is usually structured in terms of two mutually exclusive hypotheses referred to as the null hypothesis and the alternative hypothesis denoted by$H_0$and$H_1$respectively.

Two types of error occur in hypothesis testing; these are type I error and type II error. Type I error occurs if$H_0$is rejected when it is true. The probability of a type I error is the conditional probability, \emph{P (reject}$H_0$$|H_0$\emph{is true)} and is denoted by$\alpha$.

Hence,

$\alpha$ = \emph{P (reject}$H_0$$|H_0$\emph{is true)} and

$1-\alpha$ =\emph{ P (accept}$H_0$$|H_0$\emph{is true)}

Type II error if $H_0$is accepted when it is false. Its probability is denoted by the symbol$\beta$, where

$\beta$ = \emph{P (accept}$H_0$$|H_0$\emph{is false)} and

$1-\beta$ =\emph{ P (reject}$H_0$$|H_0$\emph{is false)} called power of the test

\textbf{Types I and II error can be explained as follows:}

## Tests on a Population Proportion
It is often necessary to test hypotheses on a population proportion. For example, suppose that a random sample of size \emph{n} has been taken from a large population and that$X(\le n)$observations in this sample belong to a class having a particular characteristic of interest. Then $\hat{P}=X/n$is a point estimator of the proportion of the population \emph{p }that belongs to this class. Note that \emph{n }and \emph{p} are the parameters of a binomial distribution. Recall that the sampling distribution of$\hat{P}$is approximately normal with mean $p$and variance$p(1-p)/n$, if $p$is not too close to either \emph{0} or \emph{1} and if \emph{n} is relatively large.

In many engineering problems, we are concerned with a random variable that follows the binomial distribution. For example, consider a production process that manufactures items that are classified as either acceptable or defective. It is usually reasonable to model the occurrence of defectives with the binomial distribution, where the binomial parameter \emph{p }represents the proportion of defective items produced. Consequently, many engineering decision problems include hypothesis testing about \emph{p}.

Consider testing

\[
H_0:p=p_0
\]

\[
H_1:p\ne p_0
\]

For large samples, the normal approximation to the binomial with the test statistic

\[
Z=\frac{X-np_0}{\sqrt{np_0(1-p_0)}}=\frac{X/n-p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}=\frac{\overline{P}-p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}
\]

may be used.

This presents the test statistic in terms of the sample proportion instead of the number of items \emph{X }in the sample that belongs to the class of interest.

## Regression and Correlation Analysis

## Introduction
Many problems in engineering and science involve exploring the relationships between two or more variables. \textbf{Regression analysis }is a statistical technique that is very useful for these types of problems. For example, in a chemical process, suppose that the yield of the product is related to the process-operating temperature. Regression analysis can be used to build a model to predict yield at a given temperature level. This model can also be used for process optimization, such as finding the level of temperature that maximizes yield, or for process control purposes. Other examples are, studying the relationship between blood pressure and age, the concentration of an injected drug and heart rate etc.

Regression analysis is concerned with the study of the dependence of one variable, the dependent variable, on one or more other variables, the independent or explanatory variables with a view to estimating and predicting the (population) mean or average of the former (dependent) in terms of the known or fixed (in repeated sampling) values of the latter (independent).

Very often in practice, a relationship is found to exist between two (or more) variables and one wishes to express this relationship in mathematical form by determining an equation connecting the variables.

Correlation analysis, on the other hand, is concerned with measuring the strength of the relationship between variables. When we compute measures of correlation from a set of data, we are interested in the degree of the \emph{correlation} between variables.

## The Regression Model
In the typical regression problem, the researcher has available for analysis a sample of observations from some real or hypothetical population. Based on the result of his analysis of the sample data, he is interested in reaching decisions about the population from which the sample is presumed to have been drawn. It is important that the researcher understand the nature of the population in which he is interested.

In the simple linear regression model two variables \emph{X} and \emph{Y}, are of interest. The variable \emph{X} is usually referred to as the independent variable, while the other variable, \emph{Y} is called the dependent variable; and we speak of the regression of \emph{Y} on \emph{X}.

The following are the assumptions underlying the simple linear regression model.

  - Values of the independent variable \emph{X} are fixed.
  - The variable \emph{X} is measured without error.
  - Values \emph{Y} are normally distributed
  - The \emph{Y} values are statistically independent.
  - The variances of the subpopulations of \emph{Y} are all equal.

\[
V(Y|x)=V(\alpha+\beta x+e)=V(\alpha+\beta x)+V(e)=0+\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}
\]

  - The means of the subpopulations of \emph{Y} all lie on the same straight line, this is the assumption of linearity.

$E(Y|x)=\mu_{Y|x}=\alpha+\beta x$ (1.0)

These assumptions may be summarized by means of the following equation which is called the simple linear regression model because it has only one independent variable or \textbf{regressor}:

$y=\alpha+\beta x+e$ (1.1)

where $\alpha$and$\beta$(slope and intercept) are called population regression coefficients, \emph{e} is called the \emph{\textbf{error term }}with \textbf{mean zero} and variance $\sigma^{2}$. The random errors corresponding to different observations are also assumed to be uncorrelated random variables.

The results of \emph{n} observations of the set of random variables \emph{X} and \emph{Y} can be summarized by drawing a scatter diagram. A straight line passing closely to the points may be drawn. The main problem arises when the points do not all lie exactly on the straight line, but simply form a cloud of points around it. Thus, it may be possible by guess work to draw quite a number of lines each of which will appear to be able to explain the relationship between \emph{X} and \emph{Y}. We shall consider finding a best fit line. Such a line will then be used as a model relating the random variable \emph{Y} with the random variable \emph{X}.

Suppose that we have \emph{n }pairs of observations$(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\dots,(x_n,y_n)$. The following figure shows a typical scatter plot of observed data and a candidate for the estimated regression line.

The estimates of $\alpha$and$\beta$should result in a line that is (in some sense) a ``best fit'' to the data. The German scientist Karl Gauss proposed estimating the parameters$\alpha$and$\beta$in Equation 1.1 to minimize the sum of the squares of the deviations in the diagram. We call this criterion for estimating the regression coefficients the \textbf{method of least squares. }Using Equation 1.1, we may express the \emph{n }observations in the sample as

\[
y_i=\alpha_i+\beta x_i+e_i
\]

\[
i=1,2,\dots,n
\]

and the sum of the squares of the deviations of the observations from the true regression line is

\[
L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y-\alpha-\beta x_i)^{2}
\]
 (1.2)

The least squares estimators of$\alpha$and$\beta$, say $\hat{\alpha}$and$\hat{\beta}$, must satisfy

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\alpha}=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y-\hat{\alpha}-\hat{\beta}x_i\right)=0
\]

(1.3)

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta}=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y-\hat{\alpha}-\hat{\beta}x_i\right)x_i=0
\]

Simplifying these two equations yields

\[
n\hat{\alpha}+\hat{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i
\]

(1.4)
\[
\hat{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i+\hat{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i
\]

Equations 1.4 are called the least squares normal equations. The solution to the normal equations results in the least squares estimators$\hat{\alpha}$and$\hat{\beta}$.

The least squares estimates of the intercept and slope in the simple linear regression model are

$\hat{\alpha}=\overline{y}-\hat{\beta}\overline{x}$ (1.5)

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

\[
=\frac{n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i\right)}{n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\right)^{2}}
\]
 (1.6)

Equation 1.6 can also be written as

\[
\hat{\beta}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i-n\overline{x}\overline{y}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}-n\overline{x}^{2}}
\]

The estimated regression line is therefore

\[
\hat{y}=\hat{\alpha}+\hat{\beta}x
\]

## Method of Least Squares
We shall now find a and b, the estimates of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ so that the sum of the squares of the residuals is a minimum. The residual sum of squares is often called the Sum of Squares of Errors (SSE) about the regression line. This minimisation procedure for estimating the parameter is called the ``methods of least squares''. Hence we shall find a and b so as to minimise

\[
SSE=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\hat{y}_i\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-a-bx_i)^{2}
\]

Differentiating SSE with respect a and b, we have

\begin{align*}\frac{\partial(SSE)}{\partial a}&=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-a-bx_i)\\ \frac{\partial(SSE)}{\partial b}&=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-a-bx_i)x_i\end{align*}

Setting the partial derivative equal to zero and rearranging the terms, we obtain the equation (called the normal equations)

\begin{align*}na+b\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i\qquad\dots(1)\\[6pt] a\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i+b\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i\qquad\dots(2)\end{align*}

Solving for a and b from (1) and (2)

[Figure: see the PDF notes]

Equations (1) and (2) can also be solved using matrices as:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}n & \sum x\\ \sum x & \sum x^{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a\\ b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum y\\ \sum xy\end{bmatrix}
\]

\[
\begin{bmatrix}a\\ b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}n & \sum x\\ \sum x & \sum x^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\sum y\\ \sum xy\end{bmatrix}
\]

Besides assuming that the regression of y and x is a linear function having the form E(Y|X) = $\alpha$ + $\beta$x we have made three further assumptions which may be summarised as follows:

  - \textbf{Normality:} We have assumed that each variable y\textsubscript{i}has a normal distribution.

2.\textbf{ Independence:} We have assumed that the variables y\textsubscript{i}....y\textsubscript{n} are independent.

  - \textbf{Homoscedasticity}:- We have assumed that the variable y\textsubscript{i}....y\textsubscript{n} have the same variance $\sigma$\textsuperscript{2}. This assumption is called the assumption of homoscedasticity. In general, it is said that random variables having the same variance are homoscedastic, and random variables having different variance are heteroscedastic.

## Correlation Analysis
Closely related but conceptually very much different from regression analysis is \textbf{correlation analysis,} where the primary objective is to measure the \emph{strength or degree of linear association} between two variables. The \textbf{correlation coefficient} measures this strength of (linear) association. For example, we may be interested in finding the correlation between smoking and lung cancer; between scores on mathematics and fluid mechanics examinations, between high school grades and college grades etc. In regression analysis, as already noted, we are not primarily interested in such a measure. Instead, we try to estimate the average value of one variable on the basis of the fixed values of another variable.

The population correlation coefficient between two random variables, \emph{X }and \emph{Y }is defined as

\[
\rho=\frac{E\big[X-E(X)\big]\big[Y-E(Y)\big]}{\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(Y)}}=\frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(Y)}}=\frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}
\]

where $\sigma_{XY}$is the covariance between variables \emph{X} and \emph{Y}, $\sigma_X$ and $\sigma_Y$are the standard deviations of \emph{X} and \emph{Y }respectively. It is possible to draw inferences about the correlation coefficient$\rho$using its estimator, the sample correlation coefficient, \emph{r.} ``r'' is the correlation coefficient between ``n'' pairs of observations whose values are \emph{(X}\emph{\textsubscript{i}}\emph{, Y}\emph{\textsubscript{i}}\emph{) }and is given by

\[
r=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i-n\overline{x}\overline{y}}{\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}-n\overline{x}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i^{2}-n\overline{y}^{2}\right)}}
\]

\[
=\frac{n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i\right)}{\sqrt{\left(n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\right)^{2}\right)\left(n\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i\right)^{2}\right)}}
\]

\textbf{Properties of }\emph{\textbf{r}}\textbf{:}

  - It is symmetrical in nature (the two variables are treated symmetrically). That is, there is no distinction between the dependent and independent variables.
  - Both variables are assumed to be random.
  - It can be positive or negative, the sign depending on the sign of the term in the numerator which measures the sample co variation of the two variables.
  - It lies between the limits of -1 and +1; that is, $-1\le r\le+1$.
  - If \emph{X} and \emph{Y} are independent, the correlation coefficient between them is zero but if \emph{r=0} it does not mean that the two variables are independent.
